Types of Chemical Reactions

From combination to redox, every type of chemical reaction follows a recognisable pattern. Learn to identify each type, write the correct equation, and answer exam questions confidently.

Why Classifying Reactions Matters

Chemists classify reactions into types because each type follows predictable rules. Once you recognise the type of reaction, you can predict what products will form — even for substances you have never seen before. This skill is tested directly in exams and forms the foundation for organic chemistry and industrial processes at higher levels.

At Class 6 to O Level, you need to know six main types: combination, decomposition, displacement (single replacement), double displacement, combustion, and redox. Each has a characteristic pattern in its equation.

1. Combination Reactions

In a combination reaction (also called a synthesis reaction), two or more substances join together to form one new product. The general pattern is:

A + B → AB

The reactants can be elements, compounds, or a mix of both. Combination reactions often release energy as heat or light.

Examples

  • Iron + sulfur → iron sulfide: Fe + S → FeS
  • Magnesium burning in oxygen: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO
  • Hydrogen and oxygen forming water: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
  • Carbon dioxide dissolving in water: CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ (carbonic acid)

How to recognise this type: one product is produced from two or more reactants. If you see a single arrow leading to a single substance on the right, it is almost certainly combination.

2. Decomposition Reactions

A decomposition reaction is the reverse of combination — one compound breaks down into two or more simpler substances. Energy is usually needed to drive decomposition, most often in the form of heat (thermal decomposition) or electricity (electrolysis).

AB → A + B

Examples

  • Thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (requires strong heating)
  • Decomposition of hydrogen peroxide: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂ (manganese dioxide as catalyst)
  • Electrolysis of water: 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂
  • Decomposition of copper carbonate: CuCO₃ → CuO + CO₂ (green solid turns black)

A classic exam clue: if the question says "heated strongly" or "passed electricity through," suspect decomposition.

3. Displacement Reactions (Single Replacement)

In a displacement reaction, a more reactive element pushes a less reactive element out of its compound. This follows the reactivity series — an element can only displace another element that sits below it in the series.

A + BC → AC + B

Examples

  • Iron displacing copper: Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu (iron is more reactive than copper)
  • Zinc displacing hydrogen from acid: Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂
  • Magnesium displacing iron: Mg + FeSO₄ → MgSO₄ + Fe
  • Copper with silver nitrate: Cu + 2AgNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2Ag

The visual clue in a lab: if you drop a piece of metal into a solution and the solution changes colour — or a new solid appears on the metal's surface — a displacement reaction has occurred.

4. Double Displacement Reactions (Precipitation)

In a double displacement reaction, two compounds swap their ions to form two new compounds. One of the products is often an insoluble precipitate that falls out of solution as a solid.

AB + CD → AD + CB

Examples

  • Lead nitrate + potassium iodide: Pb(NO₃)₂ + 2KI → PbI₂ + 2KNO₃ (bright yellow precipitate of lead iodide)
  • Barium chloride + sodium sulfate: BaCl₂ + Na₂SO₄ → BaSO₄ + 2NaCl (white precipitate of barium sulfate)
  • Silver nitrate + sodium chloride: AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl + NaNO₃ (white precipitate of silver chloride)

Precipitation reactions are used as tests for specific ions in the laboratory. If a white precipitate forms when you add barium chloride to a solution, sulfate ions are present.

5. Combustion Reactions

Combustion is the rapid reaction of a substance with oxygen, producing heat and light. Most combustion reactions involve fuels (hydrocarbons) burning in oxygen. There are two types: complete and incomplete combustion.

Complete Combustion

When there is enough oxygen, a hydrocarbon burns completely to produce only carbon dioxide and water.

  • Methane burning: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O
  • Propane burning: C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O

Incomplete Combustion

When oxygen is limited, combustion is incomplete and produces carbon monoxide (CO) and/or carbon (soot) instead of carbon dioxide. Carbon monoxide is colourless, odourless, and highly toxic — this is why poorly ventilated gas heaters are dangerous.

  • Methane with limited oxygen: 2CH₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO + 4H₂O

6. Redox Reactions (Oxidation and Reduction)

Redox stands for reduction-oxidation. These reactions involve the transfer of electrons between substances. Oxidation and reduction always happen together — you cannot have one without the other.

  • Oxidation: loss of electrons (or gain of oxygen, or loss of hydrogen).
  • Reduction: gain of electrons (or loss of oxygen, or gain of hydrogen).

The memory trick: OIL RIG — Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons).

Examples

  • Iron rusting: 4Fe + 3O₂ + 6H₂O → 4Fe(OH)₃ — iron is oxidised by oxygen.
  • Copper oxide reduced by hydrogen: CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O — hydrogen reduces copper oxide; oxygen is removed from copper.
  • Zinc + copper sulfate displacement: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ (zinc oxidised); Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (copper reduced).

Fully Worked Exam Examples

Example 1 — Identify the Reaction Type

Question: Zinc powder is added to copper sulfate solution. A brown solid forms and the blue solution turns colourless. Identify the reaction type and write a word equation.

Reaction type: Displacement (zinc is more reactive than copper and displaces it).

Word equation: Zinc + Copper sulfate → Zinc sulfate + Copper

Observation clue: Brown solid = copper metal depositing; blue colour fading = Cu²⁺ ions being used up.

Example 2 — Complete Combustion

Question: Write the balanced equation for the complete combustion of ethane (C₂H₆).

Unbalanced: C₂H₆ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

Balance carbon: 2CO₂

Balance hydrogen: 3H₂O

Balance oxygen: 2 + 3/2 = 7/2 — multiply everything by 2 to remove fraction.

Balanced: 2C₂H₆ + 7O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O

Example 3 — Thermal Decomposition

Question: Green copper carbonate is heated strongly. Describe what you observe and write a balanced equation.

Observation: The green solid turns black (copper oxide forms). A colourless gas is produced (carbon dioxide) which turns limewater milky.

Equation: CuCO₃ → CuO + CO₂

Example 4 — Redox Identification

Question: In the reaction CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O, identify what is oxidised and what is reduced.

Copper oxide (CuO): loses oxygen → reduced (copper is the oxidising agent's partner).

Hydrogen (H₂): gains oxygen → oxidised (hydrogen is the reducing agent).

Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

  • Confusing single and double displacement. Single displacement: one element replaces another. Double displacement: both compounds swap partners.
  • Forgetting the reactivity series. A metal can only displace a metal below it. Copper cannot displace zinc — always check.
  • Writing CO₂ for incomplete combustion. Incomplete combustion produces CO, not CO₂. Know the difference and the conditions that cause each.
  • Mixing up oxidation and reduction. Use OIL RIG every time until it is automatic.
  • Leaving equations unbalanced. Count atoms on both sides before writing the final equation.

Exam Tips

  • Memorise the general patterns (A+B→AB, AB→A+B, etc.) — they help you write equations for unfamiliar substances.
  • Use observations as clues — colour change, precipitate, gas, temperature rise all point to specific reaction types.
  • For combustion questions, always check whether oxygen is limited or excess before deciding what products form.
  • For redox questions, identify electron transfer or oxygen movement rather than trying to memorise lists.
  • In displacement questions, always justify your answer by referring to the reactivity series.

Quick Summary

  • Combination: A + B → AB (two substances join into one).
  • Decomposition: AB → A + B (one substance breaks into two or more).
  • Displacement: A + BC → AC + B (more reactive element replaces less reactive one).
  • Double Displacement: AB + CD → AD + CB (ions swap; often forms a precipitate).
  • Combustion: fuel + oxygen → CO₂ + H₂O (complete) or CO + H₂O (incomplete).
  • Redox: electron transfer; OIL RIG — oxidation is loss, reduction is gain.

Classifying reactions correctly is the first step to writing accurate equations. Practise with different examples until identifying the type from the equation pattern becomes second nature.
